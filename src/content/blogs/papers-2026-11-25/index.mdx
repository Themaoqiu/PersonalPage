  arxivUrl={"https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29338"}
  hjfyUrl={"https://hjfy.top/arxiv/2611.29338"}
  summary={"Recently, GPT-6 Astra demonstrates strong generalization in robot control, yet limited disclosure of its training leaves open how such capabilities can be reproduced. We introduce nanoAstra, an approach to training vision-language models (VLMs) for generalizable manipulation that combines semantic action modeling with capability-preserving supervised fine-tuning (CP-SFT). nanoAstra represents robot behavior as semantic steps composed of reusable, parameterized action primitives, guided by explicit progress descriptions. Meanwhile, CP-SFT uses an explicit robot-specific system prompt to constrain manipulation outputs to a structured action space, aiming to focus adaptation on robot-specific contexts and promote specialization while minimizing interference with broader pretrained capabilities. With this formulation, we train nanoAstra on more than 5,000 hours of robot data together with general VQA data.   summary={"Most world models for future prediction prioritize aestetic quality of generated videos, but offer only weak physical grounding and limited geometric consistency over time, due to the lack of explicit 4D modeling.   title={"Motion Mamba 2.0: Autoregressive Diffusion Mamba for Long-Horizon Humanoid Control"}
  arxivUrl={"https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29897"}
  hjfyUrl={"https://hjfy.top/arxiv/2611.29897"}
  summary={"Long-horizon, real-time controllable motion generation is essential for interactive animation and humanoid robotics, where an agent must respond to changing instructions and spatial goals while moving. Existing autoregressive diffusion methods generate motion incrementally, but bounded attention caches limit access to distant history, while repeated denoising introduces substantial inference overhead. We present Motion Mamba 2.0, a Mamba-based root-body diffusion architecture that integrates local attention, resumable recurrent states, and persistent content memory (PCM) for long-horizon controllable motion generation. PCM transforms compressed motion history from Mamba recurrent states, together with state changes and text-conditioned features, into dynamically updated content slots accessible to local attention. To enable efficient inference, we further develop an execution scheme tailored to this architecture, combining CUDA Graph replay, fused kernels, and execution-flow simplification while preserving the canonical history update. Evaluations on retargeted HiPHI demonstrate accurate terminal control over rollouts of up to 39.52 s, while short-horizon comparisons on BONES Seed and retargeted HumanML3D show that the model also retains strong spatial control and text-motion matching. On an RTX 4090, the optimized execution reduces median complete-window latency from 1685.35 to 276.14 ms, achieving a median paired speedup of 6.138$\\times$ while preserving identical outputs and committed states in the tested rollouts. Moreover, we conduct extensive real-world evaluation on the Unitree G1, demonstrating sequential text-guided action composition, expressive motion, and interactive locomotion through keyboard and mouse control."}

  arxivUrl={"https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30989"}
  hjfyUrl={"https://hjfy.top/arxiv/2611.30989"}
  summary={"AI systems can now operate Blender, follow instructions in complex video games, and manipulate objects with robot arms. What range of physical goals can today's AI systems achieve autonomously? Answering this question requires testing systems on tasks that demand different combinations of perception, memory, planning, and action. We introduce \\textbf{WorldLeague}, a benchmark that compares AI systems on a common set of \\textbf{60 simulated robot tasks} by measuring progress toward each task's physical goal from the robot's trajectory. Robots explore scenes to gather evidence in \\textbf{WorldSeek}, rearrange objects in \\textbf{WorldShape}, and adapt to changes in \\textbf{WorldShift}. In WorldLeague, vision-language models (VLMs) choose targets and motions, while vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world-action models (WAMs) use their native controls. For each of six capability dimensions, we measure how much progress a system makes toward its goals (Interactive Score) and how often it achieves them (physical success rate). Together, these measures form the system's \\emph{Physical Agency Profile}. Among 13 representative AI systems, Claude Fable 5.2 achieves 13 of the 60 goals and earns the highest mean Interactive Score (28.58/100). Fable leads in four dimensions, while GPT-6.1-Sol and GPT-6-Astra tie for first on rule-following tasks. Even when VLMs answer every final question correctly, they receive an Interactive Score of zero in 71.8\\% of those episodes. WorldLeague gives Embodied AI, Physical AI, and Agentic AI a shared basis for comparing what AI systems achieve autonomously, from partial progress toward a physical goal to full goal completion. Project Webpage: https://xyangzhou.github.io/WorldLeague"}

<PaperCard
  title={"Embo-Harness: Towards Understanding Frontier-Model-as-Policy Harness"}
  arxivUrl={"https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31201"}
  hjfyUrl={"https://hjfy.top/arxiv/2611.31201"}
  summary={"Frontier multimodal models exhibit extraordinary generalization in generating robot actions from observations and instructions, enabling a new paradigm in which they serve directly as policies within closed-loop robotic harnesses. However, the design principles of such harnesses, and the capabilities underlying their strong performance, remain unclear. We present Embo-Harness, a framework for systematically studying frontier-model-as-policy harnesses. We investigate four components of the robotic agentic loop, namely, prompting, planning, action execution, and tool use, and distill six empirical findings into a practical recipe. This recipe prioritizes interfaces that exploit the model's native visual reasoning and tool-use capabilities over handcrafted plans, skills, or action priors, and identifies effective choices for action representation, chunking, reasoning effort, visual observations, short multi-action sequences, and policy refinement. On RoboLab-120, Embo-Harness achieves an 83.3\\% success rate, outperforming the strongest reported baseline by 26.2 percentage points. It also succeeds in 15 of 20 zero-shot trials across four real-world manipulation tasks. Beyond performance, controlled interventions indicate that the studied model depends more strongly on accurate spatial target estimation than on explicit low-level joint or trajectory generation. These results provide both a practical recipe for building strong frontier-model-as-policy harnesses and a clearer account of the capabilities that drive their performance."}

  arxivUrl={"https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12164"}
  hjfyUrl={"https://hjfy.top/arxiv/2611.12164"}
  summary={"Code localizers are usually trained to predict the files a reference patch edits, but the code a fixer needs to read is not the same as the code it changes. We propose Fixer-Guided Localization (FGL): the localizer proposes several localizations, a frozen fixer scores each by how likely it makes a verified patch, and the localizer is trained on these scores with weighted supervised fine-tuning, without running tests and without reinforcement learning. Training also changes how the agent works: trained agents explore about three times more than the base model and write far fewer wrong patches, and the single agent solves up to 11 of 78 tasks, against 8 for the base model. An analysis of 13 evaluation runs identifies what limits a 4B agent under an 8-minute budget (time, repetition loops and edit quality) and gives practical lessons for training small coding agents. <PaperCard
  title={"AutoPILOT: A Visual Agent Harness for Real-Time Gameplay"}
  arxivUrl={"https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28202"}
  hjfyUrl={"https://hjfy.top/arxiv/2611.28202"}
  summary={"Multimodal foundation models can play video games well when the game waits for them, but often fail when it keeps running. We find that much of this gap lies in the harness, which decides what the model sees, what it remembers, and how its outputs become actions over time. Existing harnesses such as VISTA improve the first two, yet turn each model call into at most one action, so the agent acts only as often as its model thinks. We introduce AutoPILOT, which changes the third: in each model call, the model can send a primitive action, start an open-loop macro, or write a closed-loop controller that keeps playing from the screen without further model calls until it hands control back. To evaluate agents in real-time games, we build AFK-Bench, 120 diverse browser games grouped by how they respond to inaction and playable in real time or with the clock paused. With GPT-6.1 Sol under the same budgets, AutoPILOT reaches 68.6% real-time success against 38.3% for VISTA, and on GameWorld it keeps 80.0% in real time while VISTA drops from 84.1% to 42.9%. Its gains hold across models, and it reaches VISTA's final success rate at a fifth of its API cost. Our results suggest that how a harness turns model outputs into actions over time is a central yet underexplored part of agent design, and we hope AutoPILOT and AFK-Bench will serve as a foundation for agents in a world that does not wait."}

<PaperCard
  title={"Composable Soft-Prompt Distillation of RL Experts: Toward Continual Post-Training"}
  arxivUrl={"https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27376"}
  hjfyUrl={"https://hjfy.top/arxiv/2611.27376"}
  summary={"RL post-training typically produces domain experts that must then be consolidated into one model, and as new experts keep arriving, this consolidation becomes a continual process. Weight merging and multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) must re-consolidate the whole model whenever an expert is added, and can erode capabilities outside the target domains. We propose Composable Soft-Prompt Distillation (CSPD), which keeps the pre-RL model frozen, distills each RL expert independently into a soft prompt, and composes experts by concatenating their prompts without joint training. A single soft prompt suffices to distill an RL expert, recovering at least 90% of the teacher's gain in 22 of 23 math, code, and agent settings. Independently trained prompts compose well. In a multi-expert setting, the average rises with every expert added, and the full composition matches MOPD (67.83 vs. 67.79). The composition generalizes best across six held-out benchmarks. Input-space adaptation adds little serving overhead, keeping throughput within 1.5% of the unadapted backbone and reaching 2.40x that of rank-4 LoRA. These results point toward continual post-training on a fixed backbone."}

  summary={"Current leading video generation models can synthesize highly realistic videos, while still frequently violating physical and logical constraints in multi-step reasoning and planning, even after training with large-scale reasoning data or existing diffusion RL methods. <PaperCard
  title={"World Models as Native Latent Action Models"}
  arxivUrl={"https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30898"}
  hjfyUrl={"https://hjfy.top/arxiv/2611.30898"}
  summary={"Large and diverse video sources, such as egocentric human video and heterogeneous robot recordings, carry rich dynamics but come either without actions or without a shared action space. Latent action models (LAMs) can recover these dynamics, but they train their inverse- and forward-dynamics models (IDM, FDM) from scratch, trading off pixel reconstruction against latent capacity and accuracy. Pretrained video world models natively generate high-fidelity dynamics, yet represent them densely, entangled across all layers. Viewing the world model as a LAM, we show that these dynamics can be extracted with minimal changes to the model. Native LAM operates one pretrained video diffusion transformer as both IDM and FDM, with learnable dynamics registers as the only causal bridge between the two, and trains on counterfactual temporal pairs that differ in motion alone, so that the registers distill a video's dynamics into a compact native latent action. We further propose a pretraining paradigm for vision-language-action (VLA) policies, Pretraining Actions from World models (PAWlicy), in which Native LAM serves as a dynamics teacher on large-scale video; unlike world action models, it never generates video. Native latent actions outperform existing LAMs on the LARY benchmark in both action classification and control regression, and replay the same motion on new robots, in new scenes, and from human hands to robots, although no transfer objective is used in training. With a 3.8B model, PAWlicy reaches a 93.6% average success rate on RoboTwin 2.0 trained with domain-randomized demonstrations, the highest among VLAs. On RoboDojo, it is competitive on every dimension we evaluate, and its Precision, evaluated by us, is on par with the leading leaderboard entries. On real robots, it improves over the pi_0.5 baseline on both tasks. Both stages use only public data and a publicly released world model."}
